Engine wiring harnesses are a critical component located in the engine bay of automobiles, typically routed along the engine block and secured to structural mounting points. These harnesses carry electrical signals between the engine control unit and key sensors and actuators including fuel injectors, oxygen sensors, temperature sensors and ignition systems. In the AutoVI dataset, the specific assembly being inspected consists of a bundle of coloured wires — orange, yellow and green — that must be held together by a blue retention hoop and secured in position by two metal clips fastened on either side of the hoop. If the wires are not properly contained within this hoop and clip arrangement, the bundle is exposed to constant engine vibration which causes the individual wires to flex repeatedly at their connection points, leading to metal fatigue and eventual wire breakage. Uncontained wires can also contact hot surfaces such as the exhaust manifold, melting the insulation and causing short circuits, or get caught in rotating components causing complete harness failure. These failures manifest as intermittent sensor faults, ECU error codes, loss of safety systems such as ABS and traction control, and in severe cases engine fires — all of which pose serious safety risks and result in costly field recalls. Traditional human inspection of this assembly is unreliable because subtle defects such as a slightly displaced hoop or a missing clip are difficult to spot consistently at production line speeds, and inspector performance degrades over a shift due to fatigue. Human inspection also produces no digital record, making it impossible to track defect trends or demonstrate compliance during quality audits. An ML-based inspection system addresses all of these limitations — it applies the same detection logic consistently to every image regardless of time of day or operator, processes images in seconds without slowing the production line, generates a full audit trail of every inspection decision with anomaly scores and annotated images, and can detect subtle surface defects that the human eye routinely misses.
